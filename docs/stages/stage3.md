# Stage 3 — Baseline scripted AI

For a concept-level and implementation-level walkthrough (with diagrams), see [stage3-concepts.md](stage3-concepts.md).

## Scope

Two fixed, deterministic scripted teams that can play a full match against each other end-to-end, with no ML involved. This stage introduces the `Agent` interface every later ML approach (behavior cloning, single-agent RL, multi-agent RL) will plug into, and a minimal per-team role-assignment layer so a roster of individually-simple agents produces recognizable team shape: someone chases the ball, defenders mark, a keeper minds the goal, and the ball-carrier chooses between shooting, passing, and dribbling.

Also in scope: closing a gap `physics/reset.py` already flagged — out-of-bounds restarts (throw-ins, corners, goal-kicks) — since a scripted match that just stops dead every time the ball goes out isn't really "a full match."

Explicitly **not** in scope: formal Gymnasium observation/action spaces (Stage 4), learned tactics (Stage 5), and a tackle/dispossession mechanic (see "Known gap" below) — a chasing defender can pressure the ball-carrier but the physics kernel has no mechanism yet for them to actually win it off them mid-dribble.

## Decisions

- **One `Agent` per player, not one per team.** `Agent.act(player_id, state, role, config) -> PlayerAction` is called once per player per step. This matches the ROADMAP's `act(observation) -> action` phrasing and — more importantly — composes with every later stage: Stage 6 (behavior cloning) and Stage 7 (single-agent RL) both train *one player's* policy, so keeping the interface single-player-shaped now means nothing has to be reworked when a learned `Agent` replaces a scripted one. Team-level coordination (who chases, who marks) is handled by a separate role-assignment step (below), not by the `Agent` interface itself.

- **Observation is the raw `MatchState`, not a bespoke wrapper.** `Agent.act()` receives the actual `MatchState` plus the `player_id` it's controlling — scripted heuristics just read positions/velocities straight off it (nearest teammate, nearest opponent, ball position). Stage 4 is where a proper egocentric/normalized observation gets designed for RL; doing that work now would mean guessing what Stage 4 actually needs. Scripted agents can keep reading `MatchState` directly even after Stage 4 lands (they don't need vectorized/normalized observations to run a simple heuristic).

- **A lightweight per-team role-assignment step runs once per team per `step()` call, before agents act.** `agents/roles.py::assign_roles(state, config) -> dict[player_id, Role]` computes, for each team: who's the fixed keeper, who's the single "chaser" (the one pursuing/carrying the ball), and — for the rest — whether they're marking a specific opponent (one-to-one assignment, see below) or supporting an attack. This is deliberately **not** the Stage 5 tactics layer: it's a fixed rule-based assignment (nearest-player/greedy-matching), not a learned or configurable formation system. It exists because per-player *decentralized* role inference (each agent independently guessing "am I the marker?") can't express a proper one-to-one marking assignment without either a shared assignment algorithm (which is what this is) or accepting double-marking/unmarked-opponent bugs.
  - **Marking assignment: greedy nearest-neighbor matching**, not distance-threshold marking. Each defending outfield player is paired with an opponent outfield player by repeatedly picking the closest remaining (defender, opponent) pair until either side runs out — this is what actually prevents two defenders converging on the same attacker while a third goes unmarked, which independent "mark whoever's nearest to me" logic can't guarantee.
  - **Keeper is a fixed designation**, not computed: player `team * players_per_team` (i.e. roster index 0 for each team, following the existing `_line_up` id convention in `reset.py`) is always the goalkeeper. Real formations are a Stage 5 concern; Stage 3 just needs *a* keeper to anchor the goal.

- **Passing decisions use passing-lane obstruction, not a flat "is a teammate open" distance threshold.** Per the project's stated preference for skill-based mechanics over arbitrary thresholds (see gameplay-design memory), "how open is teammate T" is computed as the minimum distance from any opponent to the line segment connecting the ball-carrier and T — i.e. how obstructed the passing lane actually is — rather than "is the nearest opponent to T more than N meters away" (which says nothing about whether that opponent can actually intercept the pass). The carrier picks the teammate maximizing that lane clearance and passes only if it beats a "just dribble forward" baseline; otherwise they dribble. The same idea governs shot aim: a chaser in shooting range aims at whichever point along the goal mouth is farthest from the keeper's current position, rather than always at goal center.

- **Out-of-bounds restarts require tracking who touched the ball last.** Real throw-in/corner/goal-kick attribution depends on which team's player last touched the ball before it went out — information the kernel didn't previously track. Added `Ball.last_touch_team: int | None` (set on kicks, traps, *and* bounces — a deflection off a defender still counts as "the defense touched it last" for corner-vs-goal-kick purposes, even though a bounce isn't a controlled touch). `physics/reset.py` gains `restart_after_out_of_bounds(state, event, config)`, placing the ball at the boundary point where it exited:
  - **Touchline**: ball placed just inside the touchline at the exit point, awarded to whichever team *didn't* touch it last.
  - **Goal line, not a goal**: if the *attacking* team touched it last (e.g. an overhit shot), it's a goal-kick — ball placed at a fixed spot in the goal area. If the *defending* team touched it last (deflection or defensive clearance out), it's a corner — ball placed at the nearest corner arc.
  - This extends `physics/reset.py` (where `restart_after_goal` already lives) rather than adding a new module — restart-after-event is already that file's job.
  - `viz/replay.py` round-trips `last_touch_team` from the start (Stage 2 got bitten once by a new state field silently not round-tripping through replays — see `stage2.md` — so this field gets a round-trip test immediately rather than after the fact).

- **Known gap, deliberately deferred: no tackle/dispossession mechanic.** `physics/step.py` currently has no way for a non-carrier to take the ball from a carrier — once `carrier_id` is set, the ball rides with them every step regardless of how close an opponent gets (see `step.py`'s "Ball carrying" section); the only ways possession changes are a pass/shot, the ball going out of play, or a goal. This means Stage 3's marking/pressure defenders can crowd a dribbler but can never actually win the ball off them. Fixing this is a real physics-kernel addition (a new contact case alongside the existing trap-vs-bounce logic, needing its own tuning and tests) and is out of scope here — flagged in `step.py`'s module docstring as a follow-up, to be picked up as its own addendum once there's a clearer sense of what "contested" should feel like.

- **Addendum (found by actually watching a match, not by unit tests): steering needed arrival braking, not constant full acceleration.** Every role's target computation (`agents/scripted.py`) reduces to "move toward this point," and the first version of `_move_toward` always requested full `player_max_accel` straight at the target with no slowdown at all. That's fine while far away, but a player closing in on a *fixed* target (a keeper's spot, a marker's shadow position, a support anchor) would blast straight through it at max speed, get hard-braked by `player_brake_accel` once its direction reversed, overshoot the other way, and settle into a slow, visibly "pendulum"-like decaying oscillation — reported directly from watching a live/recorded match, not caught by any unit test (the existing tests only checked direction, never convergence). Fixed by capping the *desired* approach speed at `sqrt(2 * player_brake_accel * distance)` — the standard kinematic relation for "how fast could I be going and still stop exactly here at this braking rate" — so the slowdown is timed by real kinematics, not an arbitrary "start braking within N meters" constant. Far from the target this cap simply exceeds `player_max_speed` and has no effect (unchanged full-speed pursuit); only near the target does it pull the desired speed down, smoothly, to zero. A small additional fixed-radius dead-zone (`ARRIVAL_DEADZONE_M`) exists purely to kill a leftover floating-point-scale velocity chatter right at the target (steering toward a "direction" whose distance is near-zero is numerically noisy) — that one *is* an arbitrary constant, but a numerical-stability one, not a gameplay mechanic. See `test_move_toward_converges_to_a_stop_at_its_target_without_oscillating` in `tests/test_agents_scripted.py` for the regression test.

- **Addendum: the keyboard-controlled debug player needed the same braking treatment, since players have no passive friction at all.** Only `Ball.velocity` decays via `ball_friction`; a player's velocity is untouched unless something actively requests an opposing acceleration (`step.py`'s `is_braking` check). Releasing every arrow key sends a *zero* acceleration request, which doesn't oppose anything, so the human-controlled player just coasted forever at whatever speed it had — reported directly as "the player keeps an inertia that doesn't make sense." Presented two options (auto-brake-when-idle vs. a fully arcade-style instant-velocity control scheme with no momentum at all) and the user picked auto-braking, since it preserves the debug tool's original purpose of visually exercising the kernel's acceleration/braking model. Fixed in `scripts/run_match.py::_read_keyboard_action`: when no directional key is held, request `player_brake_accel` opposing the current velocity instead of zero. The dead-zone below which velocity is canceled outright (rather than approached via one more braking step) can't be an arbitrary small constant either — it has to be at least `player_brake_accel * dt` (one frame's worth of braking), or braking overshoots straight past zero and chatters forever right at the boundary, which is exactly what a first attempt at an arbitrary `0.05` constant did (caught by simulating the idle-braking case directly, the same way the `_move_toward` bug above was caught, not by inspection).

- **Addendum: restarts needed an actual enforcement mechanism, not just correct team attribution.** `restart_after_out_of_bounds` correctly *decided* who should get a corner/goal-kick from the start, but nothing stopped the *other* team — including the team that just conceded the restart — from simply walking up and touching it first; touchline restarts didn't even attribute a team at all. Reported directly as "the last to touch the ball is able to kick it," and confirmed by tracing real match replays: touchline restarts recovered by the wrong team roughly half the time, often within a single step (a player already standing next to the restart spot, since positions aren't reset — a stationary ball is always receivable per `_is_receiving`, regardless of intent). Presented two enforcement options — a role-assignment-level lock (no new physical constraints) vs. a real keep-out radius like football's ~9.15m free-kick rule — and the user picked the role-assignment lock. Implementing it correctly required going one step further than originally scoped, though: suppressing the `CHASER` role alone doesn't stop a player who *happens* to already be adjacent to the restart spot from accidentally trapping it, since trapping only depends on proximity, not role. So the enforcement is actually anchored in physics: new `Ball.restart_owner_team` field, set by every restart (touchline now awards the opposite team of `last_touch_team`, matching what corners/goal-kicks already computed); `physics/step.py::find_possessor` excludes the non-owning team from possession/contact candidacy entirely while it's set, so they can't trap, kick, or even accidentally deflect a restarted ball no matter how close they are; `agents/roles.py` additionally gives the locked-out team no `CHASER` at all, so their scripted players don't even try. The lock clears itself the instant any contact happens — which, thanks to the `find_possessor` filtering, can only ever be a legitimate touch by the entitled team.

## Done criteria

- `soccersim/agents/base.py` defines the `Agent`/`Role` interface that every future scripted/cloned/RL agent implements.
- `soccersim/agents/roles.py::assign_roles()` produces a deterministic keeper/chaser/marker/support assignment for both teams from a `MatchState`, with one-to-one marking (never two defenders assigned to the same opponent while another goes unassigned, when counts allow).
- `soccersim/agents/scripted.py::ScriptedAgent` implements chase-ball, mark-nearest (goal-side positioning on the assigned mark), pass-to-open-teammate (lane-clearance based), and basic keeper (tracks ball's line, anchored near goal) behaviors.
- Two full scripted teams (`players_per_team` a side) can play a complete match via `scripts/run_match.py` end-to-end — including throw-in/corner/goal-kick restarts — without crashing, going out of sync, or producing NaNs.
- `scripts/run_match.py` still supports a human-controlled debug player (Tab-cycled, as in Stage 2) alongside scripted teammates/opponents, and gains a flag to run fully scripted (no human input) for unattended/headless runs.
- `uv run pytest`, `uv run ruff check .`, and `uv run black --check .` all pass.
